Proving nothing was removed after the documentation completion date

AS 1215 does not ask a firm to promise its workpapers were not altered. It sets a shape the documentation must hold. The gap this page is about is that almost no firm can demonstrate that shape to an inspector except by pointing at its own system and asking to be believed.

What the standard actually says

Three requirements, quoted from AS 1215:

"A complete and final set of audit documentation should be assembled
for retention (i.e., archived) as of a date not more than 14 days
after the report release date"

"The auditor must retain audit documentation for seven years from the
date the auditor grants permission to use the auditor's report"

"Audit documentation must not be deleted or discarded after the
documentation completion date, however, information may be added."

The third sentence is the interesting one. It is not "do not change the file". It is append-only, stated in plain English a decade before anyone called it that. Deletion is prohibited; addition is expressly permitted. A revised version of AS 1215 takes effect on 15 December 2026, which is why most firms are looking at their documentation controls right now.

What it costs to be unable to prove it

The PCAOB publishes its own tally in its enforcement spotlight on improper alteration of audit documentation. As set out there: 33 disciplinary orders for failure to cooperate with an inspection and 28 for failure to cooperate with an investigation, 18 firms whose registration was revoked, and 53 individuals sanctioned, of whom 45 were barred. The Board states that a majority of those orders involved improper document alteration, and describes its own posture as "zero tolerance".

Losing registration is not a fine. A firm without PCAOB registration cannot audit an issuer. There is no larger consequence available in this market, and it has already landed on eighteen firms.

The gap

Every firm's quality manual already says documentation is not altered after the completion date. The manual is not the problem. The problem is what happens when an inspector asks the firm to show it.

What a firm can usually produce is a report from the same system that holds the workpapers, generated by the firm, attesting to the firm's own conduct. That is an assertion. It is only as strong as the trust the inspector extends to the firm and to the firm's software vendor, and eighteen revocations are what happens when that trust is withdrawn.

What ActaSeal does about it

ActaSeal sits behind the archive and turns each of those requirements into a signed record rather than a report. Concretely:

  • The archive lock. Assembling an engagement for retention is recorded as an event on a hash-chained, append-only ledger, with the report release date and the resulting documentation completion date bound into what is signed. A later claim about when the engagement was locked is checkable against the chain, not against a database row that can be updated.
  • Additions after the completion date, as the standard allows them. There is a dedicated path for this (POST /archive/engagements/{id}/workpapers/{id}/post-archive-addition), so a permitted addition is recorded as an addition, with who made it and when, and never as a modification of what was already there. Deletion has no path at all.
  • Clock trust. The lock does not rest on the server's own clock. External RFC 3161 timestamps anchor it, so "this was locked before that date" does not depend on the machine that did the locking being honest about the time.
  • Preparer, reviewer and EQR identity per workpaper, recorded at the time rather than reconstructed afterwards.
  • Retention and legal hold as configuration, with the seven-year period and any hold that extends it recorded as events.
  • A single inspection pack for one engagement, so what is handed to an inspector is one file rather than an export assembled by hand under time pressure.
  • An offline verifier the inspector runs. This is the part that changes the conversation. The verifier is published on its own under the Apache License 2.0, every packet embeds the public key needed to check it, and no call to the firm or to us is required. An inspector can verify the chain on their own laptop, years later, having never had any relationship with either of us.

What this does not do

Stated plainly, because a page that only lists strengths is not useful to anyone doing real diligence.

  • It does not prevent alteration. Anyone with sufficient access to a system can attempt to change what it holds. ActaSeal makes the attempt evident and provable to a third party. That is a different and more useful property than prevention, but it is not prevention and should not be bought as such.
  • It is not endorsed, approved or accepted by the PCAOB, or by any regulator or standard-setter. No such endorsement exists for any product. Whether your documentation satisfies AS 1215 remains your firm's judgment.
  • There is no SOC 2 Type II report. If your vendor qualification names one, ActaSeal does not meet that requirement today and we will confirm that in writing rather than offer a substitute.
  • No audit firm is running this in production yet. The code, the tests and the standards work are real and checkable. The deployment record is not, because there isn't one. The first firms to deploy get terms that reflect that, set out on the pricing page.
